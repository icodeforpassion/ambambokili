Writing Guide

How to Write a Kids Video Script and Storyboard

A strong kids video is usually simple on the surface and carefully planned underneath. A scene-by-scene script helps keep dialogue, visuals and pacing aligned.

Define the audience and outcome

Decide whether the video is for toddlers, preschool children or older primary-age viewers. Then decide what the viewer should feel or understand by the end: laugh at a joke, remember a rhyme, learn a word or follow a tiny story.

Write the idea in one sentence

If the concept needs a paragraph to explain, simplify it. For example: “A hungry parrot secretly eats the chillies it was supposed to deliver.” That sentence already gives you character, problem and comic ending.

Use a three-part structure

Start with the setup, introduce a small problem or surprise, then deliver a satisfying payoff. For songs, verses can introduce new details while a repeated chorus provides familiarity.

Write dialogue for the ear

Children hear dialogue before they analyse it. Use short phrases, natural Malayalam or English, and repeat important words. Read the script aloud to catch stiff wording.

Break the script into shots

For each shot, note: scene number, duration, location, character, visible action, spoken line, sound effect and camera framing. A 60-second Short may need only six to ten purposeful shots.

Keep one main action per shot

“The parrot flies in, opens a bag, eats chillies, reacts, falls over and sings” is too much for one generated shot. Split it into separate visual beats. This improves both AI generation and manual animation.

Plan the opening carefully

The first seconds should confirm what the title and thumbnail promised. Show the main character or action early rather than spending too long on logos and introductions.

Storyboard without being an artist

A storyboard can be stick figures, screenshots, generated keyframes or simple boxes with notes. Its purpose is decision-making, not artistic perfection. You mainly need composition, action direction and continuity.

Add continuity notes

Track which hand holds an object, where a character stands, whether a door is open and what colour an item is. These details prevent obvious jumps between generated scenes.

Turn the storyboard into a production checklist

Mark each scene as script approved, visual approved, animated, voiced, sound mixed and edited. This is especially useful when making a three-minute nursery rhyme with many small shots.
